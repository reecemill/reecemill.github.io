Geomagnetic storm forecasting · results

Can a model forecast geomagnetic storms better than NOAA?

Geomagnetic storms drag satellites down, disrupt GPS and radio, and can threaten power grids. Every day at 22:00 UTC, NOAA publishes the probability of a storm on each of the next three days. This page scores that forecast on 2022–2025, the current solar maximum, and tests four models trained on 30 years of solar wind and geomagnetic data against it.

Storms follow the solar cycle

A storm day has at least one three-hour period at Kp 5- or more (NOAA's G1 and up). About one day in eight has one, and far more near the peak of the Sun's 11-year cycle.

Brier skill is the improvement over knowing only the past year's storm rate: 0% means no better, higher is better. The models' settings were chosen on 2017–2021, and they were retrained on 1996–2021 and scored once on these years, on the same days as NOAA's forecasts.

Each model against NOAA

The difference in Brier score from NOAA's forecast on the same days, with 95% paired-bootstrap intervals: below zero is better than NOAA. The next day, every model beat NOAA, two by more than chance. Two and three days ahead, none did. NOAA's forecasters can see coronal mass ejections leave the Sun a day or more before they arrive; nothing in this data can.

What this doesn't show

  • The models learn and are tested on final, cleaned data: definitive Kp and quality-checked solar wind. NOAA forecasts in real time from preliminary Kp and live solar wind with gaps. That flatters the models, most of all the next day. A fair operational comparison needs archived real-time data.
  • NOAA forecasts a mid-latitude K index, not the planetary Kp scored here.
  • The recurrent network scored best on the test years but worst on 2017–2021, so its test result isn't taken as the best; the model chosen in advance is gradient boosting with NOAA's forecast as an input.
  • Four test years, one solar maximum.

Data: the Kp index from GFZ Potsdam; hourly solar wind from NASA's OMNI dataset; NOAA's forecasts from the Space Weather Prediction Center's daily Report of Solar-Geophysical Activity.

Method, code and all three notebooks: github.com/reecemill/geomagnetic-storm-forecasting.
