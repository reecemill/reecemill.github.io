How wrong are public satellite orbits, and how much better can you do?

Almost everyone outside a satellite's operator tracks it with a TLE, a compressed public orbit run through a simplified model called SGP4. This page measures how wrong TLEs are for ESA's Sentinel-1 radar satellites, finds where the error comes from, removes half of it with a learned correction, and compares an orbit I fitted myself with detailed physics.

SGP4's blind spot

Almost all of the error is along the direction of travel, and most of it isn't random: it's a wave with a period of half a day. SGP4 leaves out that Earth's equator is slightly oval. As Earth turns under the orbit, the two bulges pass beneath it twice a day and nudge the satellite ahead and back. Plotted against where the orbit crosses the equator, the error traces two peaks and two troughs, the same for both satellites.

Borrowing the correction for a sister satellite

Training a correction needs the satellite's own precise orbits, which most satellites don't publish. Sentinel-1A flies the same orbit as 1C, so a model trained only on 1C was tested on 1A's TLEs, February to June 2026. The wave transfers; a constant along-track offset, different for each satellite, doesn't.

Determining the orbit myself

TLEs are fitted to radar and telescope tracking with SGP4's simplified physics. To see what better physics buys, I simulated radar tracking from four ground stations, fitted an orbit to 48 hours of it with batch least squares, and predicted 72 hours ahead. My propagator models Earth's gravity field to degree 40, the Sun and Moon, and optionally a drag-like acceleration estimated from the data.

What this doesn't show

  • Two satellites in one orbit. A satellite at another altitude or inclination feels the oval equator differently.
  • The tracking is simulated from the true orbit, so it's cleaner than real radar data, and four radars watching one satellite for two days is far more attention than any object gets from real surveillance networks. The comparison shows what the physics is worth, not what an operational system would achieve.
  • Sentinel-1C makes small orbit-keeping burns every week or two. No prediction foresees them; they hurt my orbit and the TLE alike.
  • ESA published no precise orbits for Sentinel-1C from 9 to 18 June 2026, while it raised and lowered its orbit, so that period is left out.

Data: precise orbits from ESA's Copernicus Sentinel-1 mission (contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data, 2025–2026); TLE history from Space-Track, not redistributed here; the EGM96 gravity model via ICGEM; Sun and Moon from JPL's DE421 ephemeris.

Method, code and all five notebooks: github.com/reecemill/orbit-determination.
